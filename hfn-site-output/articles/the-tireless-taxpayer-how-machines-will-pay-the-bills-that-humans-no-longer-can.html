The Tireless Taxpayer: How Machines Will Pay the Bills That Humans No Longer Can

The British public sector employs roughly 5.9 million people (ONS, Q1 2026). Every one of them ages. Every one gets sick, takes holidays, joins a pension scheme, and eventually retires on terms negotiated decades ago by a union representing people who are now dead. The pension obligations of the NHS alone run into hundreds of billions. And the workforce is not getting younger: the median age of a nurse in England is 44. Of a GP, 47.

Now imagine that the next million public-sector workers age at the rate of zero. They require no pension, no holiday pay, no sick leave, no employer’s National Insurance contribution. They work three shifts a day, every day, and when they wear out after ten or fifteen years they are recycled for parts. They do not vote. They do not complain. They never, under any circumstances, go on strike.

This is not a thought experiment. It is a fiscal forecast. The only question is the timeline.

Every Western government faces the same arithmetic: obligations that grow with demography (pensions, healthcare, social care) funded by a tax base that shrinks with demography (fewer workers, lower growth). The escape routes are closing — the debt trajectory is locked, the demographic trajectory is locked, and immigration is a treadmill that creates as many future obligations as it fills current gaps. The only input variable with room to move is productivity. And the only plausible source of the step-change needed is artificial intelligence and robotics.

If government achieved even a quarter of that efficiency gain across its 5.9 million employees, the saving would exceed £20 billion per year — more than the entire annual defence equipment budget.

I. The Immigrant Who Never Ages

Figure 1

Lifetime Net Fiscal Contribution: Immigrants vs Robots

A robot's net fiscal contribution dwarfs even high-wage immigrants because it never draws on public services

Source: OBR Fiscal Sustainability Report 2024; Migration Advisory Committee; author estimates for robot fiscal profile

The debate about robots replacing workers is almost always framed as a threat. Reframe it as immigration — because that is what it is, fiscally — and the picture inverts.

When a young migrant arrives in Britain at the age of 25, they enter the labour market, pay taxes, use public services, and — if they stay — eventually draw a state pension. The Office for Budget Responsibility’s own fiscal sustainability analysis (2024) is candid about the arithmetic: only migrants earning above the median wage are net fiscal contributors over a full lifecycle. Those earning below it — and most care workers, agricultural labourers, and service-sector employees do — cost the state more in services, benefits, and eventual pension draws than they contribute in tax. The net fiscal impact depends entirely on the wage distribution of arrivals, not on the headline number.

A robot has a different fiscal profile. It arrives at the point of manufacture. It works continuously — three shifts a day, seven days a week, fifty-two weeks a year — for a service life of roughly ten to fifteen years. During that period, its output is taxable through whatever mechanism the state chooses: corporate tax on the firm that deploys it, VAT on the goods and services it produces, or a direct automation levy pegged to the human-equivalent wage it displaces. At the end of its life, it is decommissioned and recycled. It never draws a pension. It never registers with a GP. It never sends a child to a state school. It never, at any point, transitions from net contributor to net recipient.

Goldman Sachs (2023) estimates that 300 million jobs globally are exposed to automation by generative AI alone. Scale that to the United Kingdom’s share of the OECD workforce and the figure is roughly 7-8 million exposed roles. Not all will be automated — perhaps 30-40 per cent within a decade — but the fiscal equivalent is striking: 2-3 million “workers” arriving simultaneously, all of them high-productivity, none of them ageing, none of them drawing on public services. That is more than the entire net migration into the UK over the past twenty years, compressed into a fraction of the time and carrying none of the social infrastructure costs.

The United Nations’ famous 2000 report on replacement migration calculated that the United States would need to admit 593 million immigrants by 2050 to maintain its 1995 support ratio — a number so deliberately absurd it was constructed as a reductio, not a proposal. Robots make the exercise unnecessary. They fill the gap without filling the schools, the hospitals, or the housing stock.

This is not an argument against immigration — it is an argument that immigration cannot solve the problem it is usually invoked to solve. Migrants age. Their birth rates converge toward host-country norms within a generation. The dependency ratio merely shifts forward in time. Robots break the cycle entirely, because a machine that wears out is replaced by another machine, not by a pensioner.

II. The £250 Billion Question

Figure 2

UK Government Spending: Where the Money Goes

Health, pensions, and care dominate the budget — and all three are deflateable by automation

Source: HM Treasury PESA 2026; OBR Economic and Fiscal Outlook March 2026

The National Health Service costs approximately £250 billion per year (DHSC, 2025-26). The OBR projects health spending rising from roughly 8 per cent of GDP to 13 per cent by the 2070s — driven not by waste or mismanagement but by the brute fact that older people consume more healthcare, and there will be a great many more of them.

Most of the debate about AI in healthcare fixates on diagnosis. And the results there are real: Google DeepMind’s collaboration with Moorfields Eye Hospital demonstrated that an AI system could diagnose over fifty eye diseases as accurately as world-leading ophthalmologists. AI-assisted breast cancer screening has shown a reduction in false negatives of roughly 9 per cent in large-scale trials (Lancet, 2023). Sepsis-prediction algorithms are already deployed in several NHS trusts, flagging deteriorating patients hours before clinical signs would alert a nurse.

But diagnosis, however spectacular, is a rounding error in the NHS budget. The overwhelming cost of healthcare is not working out what is wrong with someone. It is the labour of caring for them afterward.

The NHS employs approximately 1.5 million people. The social care sector employs a further 1.6 million. The majority of their working hours are consumed by tasks that are logistically complex but cognitively routine: dispensing medication on schedule, transferring patients between beds and wheelchairs, cleaning wards, preparing meals to specific dietary requirements, documenting observations, monitoring vital signs overnight. These are, without exception, tasks that fall within the current or near-term capability of robotic systems — not the humanoid robots of science fiction, but the combination of purpose-built care robots, automated dispensing systems, and AI-driven monitoring that Japanese care facilities have been piloting for a decade.

The arithmetic of care homes makes the point most starkly. A residential place in England costs roughly £800-1,000 per week at blended rates — around £47,000 per year (LaingBuisson, 2024). There are approximately 400,000 care-home residents in England (CQC, 2024). The majority of that cost is labour: the carers who wash, dress, feed, monitor, and accompany residents through each day. A robot that assumes even half of those hours — the cleaning, cooking, laundry, monitoring, and mobility assistance, leaving human carers for the irreducibly human work of companionship and complex medical judgement — halves the labour cost. At 400,000 residents, even a modest reduction is measured in billions.

Then there is prevention, which is where the fiscal case becomes almost unfair. Type 2 diabetes costs the NHS roughly £10 billion annually (Diabetes UK, 2024), and it is overwhelmingly a disease of diet. A robot that prepares every meal in a household to an appropriate glycaemic profile does not advise the patient to eat better. It simply serves food that is better. Florence Nightingale proved at Scutari that systematic data collection transformed hospital mortality from 42 per cent to 2 per cent — not through medical breakthroughs, but through hygiene, nutrition, and record-keeping applied consistently. AI does the same thing at a scale that would have made Nightingale weep with satisfaction.

III. The Flat State

There is a less visible prize, and it sits inside every government department, local authority, and NHS trust in the country.

Middle management exists because information travels slowly through human organisations. A manager’s core function — strip away the euphemisms about “leadership” and “strategy” — is to collect information from the people below them, synthesise it, and pass it upward; and to receive instructions from the people above them, translate those instructions into operational tasks, and distribute them downward. This is not a criticism of middle managers. It is a description of the information-processing constraint that makes them necessary. When the only way to know what is happening on a hospital ward, in a benefits office, or across a procurement pipeline is to ask a human to write a report, you need a lot of humans writing a lot of reports.

AI eliminates the constraint. A large language model connected to an operational database can generate the report — accurate, current, formatted to specification — in seconds. It can flag anomalies that a human reviewer would miss because humans cannot hold 10,000 data points in working memory. It can answer follow-up questions instantly, in natural language, without scheduling a meeting.

The British civil service employs roughly 510,000 people (ONS, 2025). Local government employs a further 2.1 million, the NHS 1.5 million, and the wider public sector (police, fire, education, armed forces) accounts for the remainder of the 5.9 million total. How many of those roles are, at their core, coordination and information-processing? Nobody has published a definitive study, but McKinsey’s analysis of occupational tasks (2023) suggests that 60-70 per cent of time spent on “management and administrative” activities consists of data collection, synthesis, reporting, scheduling, and compliance monitoring — precisely the tasks that generative AI automates most effectively.

History provides a precedent. When the electric telegraph reached India in the 1860s, it transformed the governance of the British Raj. Before the telegraph, a district officer in Madras or Lahore exercised enormous independent authority, because instructions from London took weeks to arrive. The telegraph collapsed that delay to hours. The East India Company’s sprawling regional bureaucracy — the administrative layer required to exercise delegated judgement — became redundant. The Raj governed a subcontinent of 250 million people with a British administrative corps of fewer than 1,000 (the Indian Civil Service at its peak). The telegraph did not replace administrators with better administrators. It replaced the need for administrators by making information flow faster than human judgement could act on it.

AI is the telegraph applied to every desk in Whitehall, every council office in England, every ward in every hospital. The firms that have already adopted it offer a preview: Klarna, the Swedish fintech, announced in 2024 that its AI assistant was performing the work equivalent of 700 full-time customer-service agents. The company’s workforce shrank from 5,000 to 3,800 — not through redundancies, but through attrition and hiring freezes. If government achieved even a quarter of that efficiency gain across its 5.9 million employees, the saving would exceed £20 billion per year — more than the entire annual defence equipment budget.

IV. Who Pays the Robot’s Taxes

Figure 3

Can AI Revenue Close the Borrowing Gap?

Even modest tax capture of AI-driven productivity gains could eliminate annual borrowing

Source: Goldman Sachs (2023) AI GDP projections; OBR borrowing forecasts; author calculations

There is a problem with this fiscal paradise, and it is the same problem that Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson identify in Power and Progress (2023): when machines replace workers, the gains accrue to capital unless the state intervenes to redirect them.

The current tax system is designed around human labour. Income tax and National Insurance contributions together account for roughly £535 billion of the government’s £1,232 billion in annual receipts (HMRC, 2025-26). If a significant share of that labour is performed by machines, the income tax base erodes. The productivity gain is real — it shows up in corporate profits, in cheaper goods, in higher GDP — but unless the state finds a way to tax machine productivity as efficiently as it taxes human labour, the fiscal benefit bypasses the Treasury entirely.

The mechanism matters less than its existence. A robot levy pegged to the displaced human-equivalent wage. An expanded corporate tax capturing a larger share of AI-augmented profits. A higher rate of VAT on goods produced with below-threshold human labour input. A capital tax on the owners of automation infrastructure. Bill Gates proposed a robot tax in 2017; South Korea quietly reduced automation tax incentives the same year. The OECD’s global minimum corporate tax of 15 per cent is a prototype, though it aims at the wrong target — it taxes where profits are booked, not where machines do the work.

The back-of-envelope arithmetic is surprisingly favourable. Goldman Sachs projects a $7 trillion cumulative boost to global GDP from AI over ten years, with productivity growth increasing by 1.5 percentage points annually. The United Kingdom accounts for roughly 3.3 per cent of global GDP. Even if only half the projected gain materialises and the UK captures its proportional share, that is approximately £95 billion of additional economic output. Tax that at a blended effective rate of 25 per cent and the yield is £24 billion — almost exactly the current annual borrowing gap that Britain’s £3 trillion debt is compounding.

Niall Ferguson argues in The Cash Nexus (2001) that the history of sovereign finance is a history of states finding new things to tax when the old base erodes: land, then trade, then income, then consumption. Taxing machine productivity is not a radical departure from that trajectory. It is the next logical step. The only question is whether governments take it proactively or reactively — and the history of fiscal innovation, as Ferguson documents, strongly favours “reactively.”

V. The Objections

The council of sceptics assembles, and their objections are reasonable enough to deserve answers rather than dismissal.

The first is unemployment. If robots and AI displace millions of workers, the human cost outweighs any fiscal saving. This is the right question asked at the wrong resolution. The transition is not from employment to unemployment but from formal employment to household production — the pattern described in The Invisible Revolution, where robots in the home progressively reduce the cost of living until a single income, or no formal income at all, sustains a higher standard of living than two incomes do today. The displacement is real. The destitution is optional, and depends entirely on whether the cost of living falls faster than wages.

The second is capability. No robot in 2026 can provide unsupervised care to a vulnerable adult. This is true, and it will remain true for physical care tasks requiring fine motor control, emotional intelligence, and clinical judgement for at least another decade. But the fiscal prize does not require humanoid robots in care homes tomorrow. It requires AI in NHS administration today — which is already deployed — and purpose-built assistive robots for the heavy, routine, physical tasks (cleaning, cooking, lifting, monitoring) that consume the majority of care-labour hours. The sophisticated work arrives later. The savings start now.

The third is political will. Governments will not restructure the tax system in time. Almost certainly true as a description of normal politics. But the alternative is not the status quo — it is sovereign-debt crisis. The 2022 gilt-market convulsion, which removed a Chancellor and then a Prime Minister within weeks, was triggered by a fiscal announcement that was trivially reckless by comparison with the structural gap the OBR now projects for the 2040s. When the bond market moves, governments discover reserves of political will they did not know they possessed. As Rapley and Heather observe in Why Empires Fall (2024), the pattern is not that empires reform in time. It is that the ones that survive reform under extreme pressure, and the ones that do not, do not.

The fourth is that this is techno-utopianism — a faith in machines solving problems that are fundamentally political. It is not. It is techno-pragmatism. The demographic trajectory is locked: the retirees of 2050 have already been born, and the workers who would support them largely have not. The debt trajectory, absent a dramatic change, is locked. Immigration is a treadmill. The only variable left is productivity, and machines are the only source of productivity growth large enough to close the gap. Calling this utopianism is like calling the lifeboat utopian because you would prefer the ship not to be sinking.

VI. The Same Story, Told Twice

There is a pleasing symmetry to the argument, once you see it.

At one end, the household robot enters the home as an appliance — a more capable Roomba, a better slow cooker — and gradually, over a decade, reduces the cost of living to the point where a single income sustains a family in comfort. Nobody notices the revolution because it looks like shopping. Each purchase is rational, each saving is modest, and the cumulative effect is transformative.

At the other end, the government discovers that the same technology solves its largest fiscal problem. The care-home robot that halves labour costs. The AI system that eliminates three layers of NHS management. The automated benefits-assessment engine that processes claims in minutes instead of months. The diagnostic algorithm that catches the cancer early enough to treat it cheaply. Each application is incremental, each saving is departmental, and the cumulative effect is a Treasury that can meet its obligations without borrowing its way to insolvency.

The consumer and the state are solving the same problem from opposite directions, using the same tool, without coordinating with each other. The household is escaping the cost-of-living crisis. The government is escaping the cost-of-governing crisis. Neither needs the other’s permission.

The machine does not vote, does not age, does not strike, and does not retire. It is, for the first time in the long history of public finance, a fiscal resource that compounds without limit and depreciates without complaint. The only question — and it is the question that will separate the countries that navigate this century from the ones that do not — is whether any government will be wise enough to deploy it before the bond market forces them to.
