# The Useful Idiots: Why Every Alliance Between the Left and Islamism Ends the Same Way

In February 2026, the British political left celebrated what they perceived as a historic triumph. In the Greater Manchester constituency of Gorton and Denton, Green Party candidate Hannah Spencer overturned a Labour majority spanning nearly a century to secure a stunning by-election victory. Green Party leader Zack Polanski and left-wing commentators hailed the win as proof that a "multicultural coalition" could defeat the establishment.

To achieve this upset, the eco-socialist Green Party aggressively courted the constituency's Muslim population — which makes up around 30% of the electorate — by capitalising on anger over the Gaza conflict, distributing campaign videos in Urdu, and portraying themselves as the ultimate anti-imperialist champions. They promised progressive revolution. Their new voters want a rather different kind of revolution.

The Green Party is staunchly progressive, officially stating they are "unequivocally pro-trans" and deeply committed to LGBTQ+ rights and intersectional feminism. Yet they have actively aligned themselves with a voting bloc that polling consistently shows holds deeply conservative social values — with large majorities opposing homosexuality, same-sex marriage, and progressive gender ideology. The left believes they are leading a progressive revolution. In reality, they are playing a role that history has a name for: the useful idiot.

The term — used during the Cold War to describe those who propagandise for a cause without comprehending its ultimate objectives — has no confirmed attribution to Lenin, despite popular belief. But the concept is precise: highly intelligent, politically aware individuals who lend their organisational apparatus, intellectual legitimacy, and street-level agitation to a movement that will, once the dust settles, turn on them. Perhaps nowhere in modern history is this pattern more devastatingly illustrated than in the recurrent alliances between secular leftist movements and Islamist factions. The historical record is unambiguous. And it is not comfortable reading for anyone on the British or European left who imagines that today's tactical coalition will end differently.

## Part 1: The Oldest Alliance

The nexus between leftism and political Islam has its roots in the pragmatic strategies of the nascent Soviet Union. During the Russian Civil War, the Bolsheviks inherited a significant Muslim minority in the Caucasus and Central Asia. To recruit them against the Western-backed Whites, Soviet leaders concluded deals with armed Islamist groups throughout the region. The Red Army utilised them to fight anti-Bolshevik forces, freeing regular units for other fronts.

This tactical synthesis culminated in the 1920 Congress of the Peoples of the East, held by the Communist International in Baku. The conference was headlined by Grigory Zinoviev, who explicitly called for "a true people's holy war against the robbers and oppressors… a holy war, in the first place against British imperialism!" The Islamist attendees met this exhortation with enthusiastic applause. Lenin continued the effort to align Muslim communities with communist goals by co-opting Islamic clerics to act as proponents of Soviet power. Stalin later reversed this — but the template was established. Anti-Western imperialism could serve as the unifying solvent, capable of temporarily dissolving the ideological barriers between historical materialism and Islamic fundamentalism.

The intellectual vulnerability of the left in these alliances stems from a failure to recognise that Islamism functions not merely as a religion, but as a competing comprehensive political system. In 1920, Bertrand Russell was among the first to spot this. In *The Practice and Theory of Bolshevism*, he argued that Communism functions as a "secular religion" and a "universal State" highly comparable to Islam. Both movements view their frontiers as strictly provisional. Both make no functional distinction between religion and politics. Both emphasise group goals over individual interests, claim absolute universalism, and seek to convert unbelievers globally. Both provide a vision of a perfectly just society on earth — the classless Communist utopia versus the restored Caliphate.

Crucially, both share a deep antipathy toward Western civilisation and individualism. There they are, united in trying to dismantle it. The problem is that what they want at the end of that process is radically different — and completely incompatible. The left envisions a secular, egalitarian, materialist order; Islamism envisions a theocratic one governed by divine law. Once they have destroyed the West, the only remaining solution is to destroy each other. The alliance is not merely fragile. It is structurally suicidal.

Islamist intellectuals cannibalised Marxist methodologies for their own ends. Sayyid Qutb, the foundational ideologue of the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, openly integrated Leninist concepts into his writings. In *Milestones* (*Ma'alim fi al-Tariq*), Qutb advocated for an "Islamic vanguard" to combat *jahiliyyah* — the state of ignorance — a concept that directly mirrors Lenin's theory of the vanguard party. By adopting the revolutionary mechanics of the left, Islamists presented themselves as fellow anti-establishment revolutionaries, effectively masking their fundamentally reactionary objectives until it was too late.

## Part 2: Iran — The Textbook Case

The 1979 Iranian Revolution serves as the historical apex of this pattern. It provides the most meticulously documented instance of a sophisticated, well-entrenched secular left willingly facilitating the destruction of the incumbent regime — and subsequently facing total annihilation.

The seeds were sown in opposition to Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. The 1953 CIA- and MI6-backed coup that overthrew Prime Minister Mohammad Mossadegh and reinstated the Shah embedded deep anti-imperialist sentiment in Iranian political culture. A decade later, the Shah's "White Revolution" alienated both the traditional clergy and the urban working class. This dual alienation pushed the radical Islamist camp, led by the exiled Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, into tactical alignment with Iran's radical left.

The intellectual bridge was built by Ali Shariati, the father of "Red Shiism." Shariati explicitly blended Islamic theology with Marxist concepts, framing the Prophet Mohammad's mission as a class struggle against the aristocracy and slave-owners. This synthesis was intoxicating for Iran's leftist youth. When anti-government demonstrations reached critical mass in 1977, a vast campaign of civil resistance was forged — communists, socialists, and Islamists side by side.

The Tudeh Party, the orthodox Soviet-aligned communist party, possessed immense organisational capacity and credibility among the working class. Its leadership adopted a highly supportive stance toward Khomeini. In a November 1979 interview with the Greek newspaper *Elevtherotipia*, Tudeh leader Noureddin Kianuri boasted of the party's favourable reception in Tehran while dismissing the more radical Fedayeen as representing "an infantile complaint." Tudeh viewed the Islamists as a temporary, "petty-bourgeois" phenomenon that would dismantle the Western-backed monarchy — completing the necessary "anti-imperialist" stage of the revolution. They would fade. The left would inherit the state.

They did not fade. Shortly after returning to Iran, Khomeini sent his son Ahmad to meet with Massoud Rajavi, leader of the Mujahedeen-e-Khalq. The offer was blunt: "If you support the Imam and oppose his opponents, all gates will be open before you." Rajavi rejected the demand for absolute subjugation. In February 1980, he announced the MEK's political platform as the main opposition to the fundamentalist clerics. That put the MEK on an immediate collision course with Khomeini.

What followed was systematic eradication. The Islamists utilised Hezbollah gangs — informal, club-wielding militias — to execute a campaign of violence and intimidation against MEK and Tudeh gatherings. By 1981, following the ouster of the moderate President Abolhassan Banisadr, the newly formed Pasdaran (Revolutionary Guards) unleashed the full force of the state. The very leftists who had suffered torture in the Shah's prisons found themselves incarcerated by the new Islamic Republic. Thousands were arrested.

The purge reached its zenith during the 1988 executions. Inside Evin Prison and Gohar Dasht, "hanging judges" presided over drumhead tribunals. Left-wing political prisoners, viewed as atheistic threats to Islamic ideology, were executed by the thousands. The Abdorrahman Boroumand Center documents state violence that claimed tens of thousands of lives, effectively wiping out the entire organisational structure of the Iranian left. The Tudeh Party, the MEK, and the Fedayeen had acted as the ultimate useful idiots. Their immense sacrifices successfully toppled their primary enemy — only to install an apparatus that proved infinitely more lethal to their own existence. As one retrospective assessment put it: the true believers are always the first to get stood up against the wall once the revolution's been won.

## Part 3: Indonesia — The Killing Fields

While Iran highlights the ideological deception of the Red-Green alliance in the Middle East, Indonesia demonstrates the danger of attempting to institutionalise incompatible forces within a formal state structure. In Indonesia, the left was not a fringe underground movement. It was the largest communist party in the non-communist world. Yet their tactical alignment with religious nationalism resulted in one of the most catastrophic massacres of the twentieth century.

Following independence from the Dutch, President Sukarno introduced "Guided Democracy" in 1959. At its heart was NASAKOM — an acronym for *Nasionalisme* (Nationalism), *Agama* (Religion), and *Komunisme* (Communism). Sukarno argued that nationalism, Islam, and Marxism were wholly compatible, presenting NASAKOM as a unifying force to battle Western neo-colonialism. The Indonesian Communist Party (PKI) enthusiastically embraced it. By the early 1960s, the PKI claimed over three million members, deeply infiltrating labour unions, peasant organisations, and intellectual circles. The PKI viewed their institutionalisation within NASAKOM as a strategic triumph — a state-sanctioned, multi-class united front behind which they could gradually transition the country toward socialism.

The mandatory inclusion of *Agama* (Religion) meant the PKI was forced into an uneasy alliance with powerful Islamic organisations, most notably Nahdlatul Ulama (NU) and its militant youth wing, Ansor. The PKI severely underestimated the deep-seated theological animosity these religious groups harboured toward the atheist foundations of Marxism. They believed shared anti-imperialist rhetoric and Sukarno's patronage would override religious friction. It was a fatal miscalculation.

On 1 October 1965, a group of left-wing military officers — the "30 September Movement" — kidnapped and murdered six senior army generals in what was widely perceived as an abortive coup. The Indonesian Army, led by Major General Suharto, rapidly seized control, laying the blame entirely at the feet of the PKI. What followed was a swift, army-orchestrated campaign of mass killing. Crucially, the military did not act alone. They actively enlisted and armed Islamic organisations to carry out the slaughter at the grassroots level.

Islamic militias, particularly Ansor, acted as the primary death squads in regions like East Java. US Consulate telegrams from Surabaya explicitly noted: "We continue to receive reports [of] PKI being slaughtered by Ansor [a Muslim militia] in many areas East Java." The death toll remains debated, but conservative estimates place the number of murdered communists and leftists between 500,000 and 1.5 million, with an additional one million imprisoned. In a matter of months, the third-largest communist party in the world was physically liquidated. TIME Magazine, in July 1966, described the suppression as "The West's best news for years in Asia." The PKI's participation in NASAKOM — their belief that they could ride the tiger of Islamic nationalism to secure state power — rendered them entirely exposed when the religious and military factions united to devour them.

## Part 4: The Pattern Repeats

The dynamic extends beyond Iran and Indonesia. In Sudan, the Sudanese Communist Party provided the intellectual and organisational backbone for the 1964 October Revolution that toppled the Abboud regime — fighting alongside the Muslim Brotherhood in a "modern forces" coalition. Once the military fell, the Brotherhood pivoted to align with traditional religious parties. They weaponised a single student's alleged blasphemy to orchestrate a manufactured moral panic, providing the political cover to outlaw the SCP in 1965. The party's leadership was later executed by the Nimeiri regime. The communists had built the revolution. The Islamists and their allies consumed it.

In the West, the magnetic pull of the Red-Green alliance remains potent. In France, politicians like Jean-Luc Mélenchon and La France Insoumise have actively courted the Arab-Muslim electorate through pro-Palestinian stances and rhetoric that aligns closely with Islamist grievances. The French state has launched inquiries into *islamo-gauchisme* — Islamo-leftism — warning that segments of the academic and activist left are effectively enabling Islamist separatism under the guise of "anti-racism." In the United Kingdom, left-wing insurgent candidates have become notorious for flirtations with Islamist groups and rhetoric sympathetic to Hamas and Hezbollah, calculating that bloc votes matter more than ideological consistency.

Localised examples of what happens next are already occurring. In Hamtramck, Michigan, an all-Muslim city council — elected with the help of progressive voters — immediately moved to ban LGBTQ+ Pride flags on city property. The progressives had provided the electoral vehicle. Once in power, the new majority enforced their own values.

The asymmetry is structural. The left dominates university campuses and labour unions. Islamism dominates the mosques — commanding a vast, spiritually obedient network that can be instantly weaponised when the tactical alliance fractures. When the shared enemy is defeated, the fragile ideological bridge collapses. The Islamist faction, armed with a divine mandate and superior grassroots mobilisation, inevitably pivots to crush their former allies. They view the atheistic or progressive tenets of the left not as a differing political opinion, but as a spiritual contagion that must be physically eradicated.

## Part 5: What Comes Next

The European left must heed the warning. The secular socialists, communists, and progressives who allied with Ayatollah Khomeini to topple the Shah believed they were ushering in an anti-imperialist utopia. Instead, once the Islamists consolidated power, the left's usefulness expired. They were systematically arrested, jailed, or executed by the thousands.

The demographic trajectory in European cities — Birmingham, Bradford, Marseille, Brussels, Amsterdam — suggests Muslim populations will continue to grow. The left's fatal assumption is that cultural conservatism is a "phase" that education and exposure will cure. History suggests otherwise. When ideological enemies unite solely out of a shared desire to tear down the existing order, betrayal is not a possibility. It is an absolute certainty.

Every alliance between the secular left and Islamism has ended the same way: the left builds the revolution, provides the organisational apparatus and intellectual legitimacy, and then gets stood up against the wall. Iran, Indonesia, Sudan — the pattern has no exceptions. The only question is whether the British and European left will learn from history, or whether they will become its next chapter.
