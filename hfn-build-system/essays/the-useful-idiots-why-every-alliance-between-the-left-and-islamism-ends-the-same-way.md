# The Useful Idiots: Why Every Alliance Between the Left and Islamism Ends the Same Way

In February 2026, the British political left celebrated what they perceived as a historic triumph. In the Greater Manchester constituency of Gorton and Denton, Green Party candidate Hannah Spencer overturned a Labour majority spanning nearly a century to secure a stunning by-election victory. Green Party leader Zack Polanski and left-wing commentators hailed the win as proof that a "multicultural coalition" could defeat the establishment.

To achieve this upset, the eco-socialist Green Party aggressively courted the constituency's Muslim population — which makes up around 30% of the electorate — by capitalising on anger over the Gaza conflict, distributing campaign videos in Urdu, and portraying themselves as the ultimate anti-imperialist champions. They promised progressive revolution. Their new voters want a rather different kind of revolution.

The Green Party is staunchly progressive, officially stating they are "unequivocally pro-trans" and deeply committed to LGBTQ+ rights and intersectional feminism. Yet they have actively aligned themselves with a voting bloc that polling consistently shows holds deeply conservative social values — not on one issue, but on every social metric polled. The left believes they are leading a progressive revolution. In reality, they are playing a role that history has a name for: the useful idiot.

The term — traditionally attributed to Lenin, though without documentary proof — describes those who lend their organisational apparatus, intellectual legitimacy, and street-level agitation to a movement that will, once the dust settles, turn on them. Nowhere in modern history is this pattern more devastatingly illustrated than in the recurrent alliances between secular leftist movements and Islamist factions. The historical record is unambiguous. And it is not comfortable reading for anyone on the British or European left who imagines that today's tactical coalition will end differently.

## Part 1: The Oldest Alliance

The nexus between leftism and political Islam has its roots in the pragmatic strategies of the nascent Soviet Union. During the Russian Civil War, the Bolsheviks inherited a significant Muslim minority in the Caucasus and Central Asia. To recruit them against the Western-backed Whites, Soviet leaders concluded deals with armed Islamist groups throughout the region. The Red Army utilised them to fight anti-Bolshevik forces, freeing regular units for other fronts.

This tactical synthesis culminated in the 1920 Congress of the Peoples of the East, held by the Communist International in Baku. The conference was headlined by Grigory Zinoviev, who explicitly called for "a true people's holy war against the robbers and oppressors… a holy war, in the first place against British imperialism!" The Islamist attendees met this exhortation with enthusiastic applause. Lenin continued the effort to align Muslim communities with communist goals by co-opting Islamic clerics to act as proponents of Soviet power. Stalin later reversed this — but the template was established. Anti-Western imperialism could serve as the unifying solvent, capable of temporarily dissolving the ideological barriers between historical materialism and Islamic fundamentalism.

The intellectual vulnerability of the left in these alliances stems from a failure to recognise that Islamism functions not merely as a religion, but as a competing comprehensive political system. In 1920, Bertrand Russell was among the first to spot this. In *The Practice and Theory of Bolshevism*, he argued that Communism functions as a "secular religion" and a "universal State" highly comparable to Islam. Both movements view their frontiers as strictly provisional. Both make no functional distinction between religion and politics. Both emphasise group goals over individual interests, claim absolute universalism, and seek to convert unbelievers globally. Both provide a vision of a perfectly just society on earth — the classless Communist utopia versus the restored Caliphate.

Crucially, both share a deep antipathy toward Western civilisation and individualism. There they are, united in trying to dismantle it. The problem is that what they want at the end of that process is radically different — and completely incompatible. The left envisions a secular, egalitarian, materialist order; Islamism envisions a theocratic one governed by divine law. Once they have destroyed the West, the only remaining solution is to destroy each other. The alliance is not merely fragile. It is structurally suicidal.

Islamist intellectuals cannibalised Marxist methodologies for their own ends. Sayyid Qutb, the foundational ideologue of the Egyptian Muslim Brotherhood, openly integrated Leninist concepts into his writings. In *Milestones* (*Ma'alim fi al-Tariq*), Qutb advocated for an "Islamic vanguard" to combat *jahiliyyah* — the state of ignorance — a concept that directly mirrors Lenin's theory of the vanguard party. By adopting the revolutionary mechanics of the left, Islamists presented themselves as fellow anti-establishment revolutionaries, effectively masking their fundamentally reactionary objectives until it was too late.

## Part 2: Iran — The Textbook Case

In November 1979, Noureddin Kianuri — the sixty-four-year-old General Secretary of the Tudeh Party, who had spent decades in exile in East Germany living under the pseudonym "Dr. Silvio Macetti" — sat for an interview with the Greek newspaper *Elevtherotipia* and boasted of his party's "favourable reception" in revolutionary Tehran. He dismissed the Islamists as a temporary, "petty-bourgeois" phenomenon. They would fade. The left would inherit the state.

The seeds of this confidence were sown in opposition to Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. The 1953 CIA- and MI6-backed coup that overthrew Prime Minister Mossadegh embedded deep anti-imperialist sentiment in Iranian political culture. The Shah's subsequent "White Revolution" alienated both the traditional clergy and the urban working class, pushing the radical Islamist camp — led by the exiled Ayatollah Khomeini — into tactical alignment with the radical left. The intellectual bridge was built by Ali Shariati, the father of "Red Shiism," who blended Islamic theology with Marxist concepts, framing the Prophet Mohammad's mission as a class struggle. When demonstrations reached critical mass in 1978, communists, socialists, and Islamists marched side by side.

Kianuri's Tudeh Party possessed immense organisational capacity among the working class. Shortly after the Shah fell, Khomeini sent his son Ahmad to meet with Massoud Rajavi, leader of the Mujahedeen-e-Khalq. The offer was blunt: "If you support the Imam and oppose his opponents, all gates will be open before you." Rajavi rejected the demand for absolute subjugation. That put the left on a collision course with Khomeini.

What followed was systematic eradication. Hezbollah gangs — informal, club-wielding militias — targeted MEK and Tudeh gatherings. By 1981, the newly formed Pasdaran (Revolutionary Guards) unleashed the full force of the state. The very leftists who had suffered torture in the Shah's prisons found themselves incarcerated by the new Islamic Republic. The purge reached its zenith during the 1988 executions, when "hanging judges" inside Evin Prison presided over drumhead tribunals. Thousands of left-wing political prisoners were executed. The Abdorrahman Boroumand Center documents state violence that claimed tens of thousands of lives.

In April 1983, Iranian state television broadcast the sixty-eight-year-old Kianuri — broken, tortured, barely recognisable — opening his forced confession with the words "Imam Khomeini, the Great Leader of the Revolution." He denounced his own life's work as "high treason" and his party's ideology as a "foreign illness." His chief intellectual, Ehsan Tabari — the philosopher who had spent a lifetime arguing that scientific materialism would triumph over religion — was paraded on the same television as having "converted to Islam" after months of solitary confinement. The Tudeh Party was destroyed. Kianuri died in Tehran in 1999, a broken man in a country he had helped deliver to the clerics.

## Part 3: Indonesia — The Killing Fields

By 1965, Dipa Nusantara Aidit had built the Indonesian Communist Party into the largest in the non-communist world. He was forty-two years old, a cabinet-level figure in Sukarno's government, and supremely confident. Over three million members. Penetration of every labour union, peasant organisation, and intellectual circle in the archipelago. The state itself had given the PKI a seat at the table.

That seat was NASAKOM — President Sukarno's formula of *Nasionalisme*, *Agama* (Religion), and *Komunisme*, which held that nationalism, Islam, and Marxism were wholly compatible. Aidit embraced it as a strategic triumph: a state-sanctioned, multi-class united front behind which the PKI could gradually transition the country toward socialism. The mandatory inclusion of *Agama* forced the PKI into an uneasy alliance with powerful Islamic organisations, most notably Nahdlatul Ulama (NU) and its militant youth wing, Ansor. Aidit severely underestimated the deep-seated theological animosity these groups harboured toward the atheist foundations of Marxism. He believed Sukarno's patronage and shared anti-imperialist rhetoric would override religious friction. It was a fatal miscalculation.

On 1 October 1965, a group of left-wing military officers — the "30 September Movement" — kidnapped and murdered six senior army generals in what was widely perceived as an abortive coup. The Indonesian Army, led by Major General Suharto, rapidly seized control, laying the blame at the feet of the PKI. What followed was a swift, army-orchestrated campaign of mass killing. Crucially, the military did not act alone. They actively enlisted and armed Islamic organisations to carry out the slaughter at the grassroots level. Ansor acted as the primary death squads in East Java. US Consulate telegrams from Surabaya noted: "We continue to receive reports [of] PKI being slaughtered by Ansor [a Muslim militia] in many areas East Java." Conservative estimates place the number of murdered communists and leftists between 500,000 and 1.5 million, with an additional one million imprisoned.

Aidit fled to Central Java when the army moved. He was captured within weeks, interrogated by military officers, and summarily executed on 22 November 1965. He was forty-two. His deputy, Njoto — a refined intellectual who played the violin and wrote poetry — was shot three weeks later, aged thirty-eight. The third-largest communist party in the world had been physically liquidated. Its leader did not even receive a trial.

## Part 4: The Pattern Repeats

Abdel Khaliq Mahjub was the most formidable communist intellectual in the Arab world. He had led the Sudanese Communist Party since 1949, championed women's rights and secular education, and provided the organisational backbone for the 1964 October Revolution that toppled the Abboud military dictatorship — fighting alongside the Muslim Brotherhood in a "modern forces" coalition. He was thirty-seven when they triumphed. The coalition had served its purpose. It would not survive the peace.

Once the military fell, the Brotherhood pivoted to align with traditional religious parties. They weaponised a single student's alleged blasphemy to orchestrate a manufactured moral panic, providing the political cover to outlaw the SCP in 1965. Mahjub's party — the very organisation that had supplied the intellectual and organisational energy for the revolution — was banned by the forces it had helped to power. When Jaafar Nimeiri seized control in 1969 and initially courted the left, Mahjub supported a counter-coup in 1971 that briefly toppled Nimeiri. It failed within three days.

On 28 July 1971, Mahjub was hanged at Kobar Prison in Khartoum. He was forty-three. His comrade Joseph Garang — the first South Sudanese man to earn a law degree, who had served as Minister of Southern Affairs — was executed beside him. The party that had provided the intellectual and organisational backbone for Sudan's democratic revolution was destroyed by the very forces it had helped to power.

In the West, the Islamists do not yet have the power to kill the left. They do not need it. They already have the power to dismantle the left's core proposals — and they are doing so, one council vote, one school protest, one silent capitulation at a time.

In France, Jean-Luc Mélenchon's La France Insoumise has courted the Arab-Muslim electorate through pro-Palestinian stances that align closely with Islamist grievances — prompting state inquiries into *islamo-gauchisme*. In Hamtramck, Michigan, an all-Muslim city council — elected with the help of progressive voters — voted in 2023 to ban LGBTQ+ Pride flags on city property. The progressives had provided the electoral vehicle. Once in power, the new majority enforced their own values.

Britain is further along this path than most of the left will admit. In 2019, hundreds of Muslim parents at Parkfield Community School in Birmingham protested against the "No Outsiders" programme — a curriculum teaching children that LGBTQ+ families exist. Labour councillors, caught between their LGBTQ+ constituency and their Muslim voting base, sided with the parents. The programme was suspended. In 2021, a teacher at Batley Grammar School showed an image of the Prophet Muhammad during a Religious Studies lesson on blasphemy. He was suspended, received death threats, and went into hiding with his family. No prominent left-wing politician defended his right to teach.

But the starkest betrayal of the left's stated principles is the grooming gangs scandal. In Rotherham, Rochdale, Telford, Oxford, Huddersfield, and Newcastle, organised networks of predominantly Pakistani-heritage men systematically raped and trafficked thousands of white working-class girls — many of them children in the care of Labour-run councils. The 2014 Jay Report found that at least 1,400 children had been sexually exploited in Rotherham alone between 1997 and 2013. Council staff and police had been aware for years. They did not act. Professor Alexis Jay's report was explicit about why: there was a "widespread perception" that senior officials conveyed messages to "downplay" the ethnic dimensions of the abuse, and staff described being given "clear direction from their managers" not to identify the ethnic origins of perpetrators for fear of being labelled racist. The 2015 Casey Report on Rotherham Council found a culture of active suppression — a council so anxious to maintain relations with the Pakistani community that it buried evidence of industrial-scale child abuse.

The left claims to be the party of women's rights, of child protection, of believing victims. Yet when those principles collided with the imperative to protect the alliance, the principles were discarded and the children were sacrificed. Not once, in one town, by one council — but systematically, across a dozen towns, over two decades, by Labour administrations that calculated the political cost of offending their Pakistani Muslim voting base was higher than the cost of ignoring the rape of working-class girls. If the left will cover up the mass sexual exploitation of children to preserve a voting coalition, there is no principle it will not abandon when the alliance demands it.

This is the early stage of the historical pattern, operating through the ballot box rather than the barrel of a gun. In Iran, Indonesia, and Sudan, the Islamists had to wait for the revolution to succeed before turning on the left. In the West, no revolution is required. They simply need sufficient demographic concentration to make the left choose between its principles and its seats. Every time a Labour councillor stays silent while LGBTQ+ education is withdrawn from a Muslim-majority school, the pattern advances. Every time a progressive candidate distributes campaign material in Urdu promising things they know their voters oppose, the bargain is struck. The left is not being killed. It is being hollowed out — surrendering its own values in exchange for votes, and calling the transaction a coalition.

## Part 5: The Electoral Bargain

The question the left cannot answer — or will not — is why it persists in an alliance that violates every principle it claims to hold. The answer is not ideological. It is arithmetic.

For most of the twentieth century, the British left did not need to court immigrant communities. It had a natural constituency: the industrial working class. Miners, steelworkers, dockers, factory hands — the backbone of the trade union movement and the foundation on which the Labour Party was built. In 1966, 69% of manual workers voted Labour. The party did not belong to the universities. It belonged to the shopfloor.

That base has been systematically destroyed. Deindustrialisation, globalisation, and the shift to a service economy hollowed out the communities that once filled Labour's ranks. The mines closed. The steelworks shut. The dockyards fell silent. And with them went millions of voters who had no reason to support a party that increasingly spoke the language of Hampstead rather than Hull. By 2019, Labour's share of C2DE voters — the skilled and unskilled working class — had collapsed to 33%, lower than the Conservatives. The party founded to represent working people could no longer win a majority of working people's votes. The Red Wall did not fall in 2019. It had been crumbling for forty years.

The left's solution was not to reconnect with its lost base. It was to replace them. Mass immigration — particularly from South Asia, the Middle East, and Africa — has created entirely new voting blocs in precisely the constituencies Labour needs to hold. First-generation immigrants overwhelmingly vote for the party that facilitated their entry and defends their right to remain. Their children inherit the affiliation. The electoral arithmetic is straightforward: every new community established in a Labour heartland is a constituency secured for a generation.

This is not conspiracy. It is observable structural incentive. The constituencies with the highest immigration consistently return the safest Labour majorities. The party has a rational, if cynical, interest in maximising the inflow — and a corresponding interest in never questioning the values of the communities it is importing. To scrutinise those values would be to threaten the pipeline.

The emotional dimension makes the strategy irresistible. The left needs to feel virtuous. Immigration allows the party to combine electoral self-interest with moral self-congratulation — simultaneously doing the "right thing" (welcoming the stranger, opposing racism) and building a replacement voter base. Any criticism of immigration can be reframed as bigotry, which conveniently also protects the electoral supply line. The grooming gangs scandal is the purest expression of this bargain: children were not sacrificed despite the left's values, but because the electoral calculus demanded silence. Virtue and self-interest aligned perfectly — and the children paid the price.

But the strategy is catastrophically short-sighted. The scale of the contradiction is measurable, and the data has not improved with time. In 2016, the ICM survey for Channel 4 found that 52% of British Muslims believe homosexuality should be illegal, 47% think gay people should not teach in schools, and 39% agree that wives should always obey their husbands. Eight years later, the 2024 JL Partners poll for the Henry Jackson Society surveyed 1,000 British Muslims and asked what social changes they wanted to see in the next twenty years. The results read like a manifesto from a parallel civilisation: 32% want Sharia law implemented in Britain. 32% want Islam declared the national religion. 52% want it made illegal to show images of the Prophet Muhammad — a direct repeal of free expression. 27% want homosexuality outlawed. 57% want halal food made compulsory in all schools and hospitals.

Consider what this means on the political spectrum the left claims to inhabit. On free speech, secular governance, LGBTQ+ rights, and the separation of religion and state, a Labour voter and a Conservative voter disagree on emphasis and on speed. They do not disagree on direction. Both accept that homosexuality is legal and should remain so. Both accept that Britain is a secular state. Both accept that citizens may criticise or satirise any religion. The British Social Attitudes survey reports that 67% of the general public now consider same-sex relationships "not wrong at all" — a figure that holds across the political mainstream. The gap between a Labour voter and a Tory voter on these questions is a few percentage points of enthusiasm. The gap between a Labour voter and the Muslim community the left is courting is a civilisational chasm. The left is culturally far closer to the right-wing Conservatives they despise than to the Islamists they have chosen as allies.

Unlike the old working class — who shared the left's secular, egalitarian instincts even when they disagreed on economics — the new voters arrive with a comprehensive, divinely ordained political system of their own. They do not need the left. They are using it. Once Muslim communities reach sufficient demographic concentration — and in Birmingham, Bradford, and Blackburn they are approaching it — they will not need Labour or the Greens as a vehicle. They will form their own parties, elect their own candidates, and implement their own values. Gorton and Denton is the tremor before the earthquake. The Green Party did not win that seat because of its climate policies. It won because a Muslim voting bloc decided to use the most convenient anti-establishment vehicle available. Next time, they may build their own.

The left will have transformed the country irreversibly — importing a civilisation that does not share a single one of its core commitments — and will then be discarded by the very electorate it created. The country will be changed. The left will be gone. And every value it held dear — secularism, feminism, LGBTQ+ rights, free expression — will be dismantled not by the right, but by the people the left invited in and called allies.

## Part 6: What Comes Next

The asymmetry is structural, and it favours the same side it has always favoured. The left dominates university campuses, newsrooms, and NGOs. Islamism dominates the mosques — commanding a vast, spiritually obedient network that can be mobilised in hours. One of these can fill a street with disciplined protestors by Friday afternoon. The other can write a strongly worded letter to *The Guardian*. When the tactical alliance fractures, as it always does, the side with the deeper roots in the community will prevail.

The demographic trajectory in European cities — Birmingham, Bradford, Marseille, Brussels, Amsterdam — suggests Muslim populations will continue to grow. The left's fatal assumption is that cultural conservatism is a "phase" that education and exposure will cure. History suggests otherwise. The 2016 ICM survey found that younger British Muslims are no more liberal on homosexuality than their parents. Integration is not softening the conservatism. It is consolidating it. When ideological enemies unite solely out of a shared desire to tear down the existing order, betrayal is not a possibility. It is an absolute certainty.

Kianuri confessed on television. Aidit was shot without a trial. Mahjub was hanged. They were not fools. They were among the most capable political operators of their generation. They simply could not conceive that their allies hated them more than their shared enemy.

Every alliance between the secular left and Islamism has ended the same way: the left builds the revolution, provides the organisational apparatus and intellectual legitimacy, and then gets stood up against the wall. Iran, Indonesia, Sudan — the pattern has no exceptions. The only question is whether the British and European left will learn from history, or whether they will become its next chapter.
